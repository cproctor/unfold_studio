Conjecture Mapping Unfold Studio's Design Language

2026-05-29

Scope note: Sandoval's [-@sandoval2014] conjecture mapping framework distinguishes the embodiments of a learning environment — its material and social features — from the mediating processes they are conjectured to activate and the learning outcomes those processes produce. A full conjecture map for Unfold Studio would cover the full range of embodiments across the platform. This document focuses on one subset: the design language — the aesthetic, typographic, interaction, and social design choices that constitute the site's ethos and the learning environment it creates. Decisions about which affordances to implement are addressed separately; what appears here is how those affordances are represented and surfaced in the UI.

High-Level Conjecture

The aesthetic, navigational, and social design of Unfold Studio—taken as a coherent whole—constitutes an ethos: a communication about what kind of place this is, who belongs here, and what kinds of engagement matter. Interface design is not decorative; “aesthetic and interface design decisions [are] pedagogically consequential, not decorative” (Proctor, 2020). The perceived affordances of the site shape rhetorical practices, which shape meanings and identities enacted there, which shape what is critically possible.

Following the permeable media framework (Proctor et al., 2025), a principled site design should invite users in, support them in making the practice part of themselves, and allow them to grow. Drawing on computational literacies (Kafai & Proctor, 2021), the design should support cognitive, situated, and critical engagement.

Conjecture: A retro-craft aesthetic that reveals rather than conceals its own construction, combined with social UI that emphasizes authentic reader-writer relationships over metrics-driven engagement, will support users in developing positive and expanded CS identities and engaging in critical computational literacies through interactive storytelling.

Conjecture Map

Design Features (Embodiments)

1. Retro Monospace Aesthetic

Typography Color Texture

What we will do: Use a fixed-width (monospace) font throughout — Monaspace Neon is confirmed as the site’s primary font. Near-monochrome palette anchored by #231F20 near-black, #ffe564 yellow (primary CTA), and #9494ff purple (secondary action). Background: repeating SVG graph-paper grid. Decorative elements: dithered bitmap images.

Mediating processes

Perceived permeability of site infrastructure. A smooth, polished interface presents a perfect surface that conceals construction. The retro aesthetic communicates instead that there is not much distance between what you see and how it works — the visible structure and texture invite users to look beneath the surface. This aligns with permeable media (Proctor et al., 2025), in which the interface invites users to extend themselves into the medium.

Evocation of pre-commercial web. The Web 1.0 aesthetic may evoke — for users who experienced it and for those who have absorbed its cultural mythology — an era of more authentic, creative, human online interaction before the commodification of attention (Zuboff, 2019). This positions Unfold Studio as an alternative to surveillance capitalism platforms.

Challenge to narrow CS identity. The site reads as a CS artifact (monospace fonts, structured layout, code-adjacent aesthetics) while the content — stories about identity, relationships, social experience — challenges the stereotype that CS is impersonal and only for certain people. This may support users who don’t see themselves in mainstream CS culture in building more expansive CS identities (Kafai & Proctor, 2021).

Precedent: p5.js web editor (editor.p5js.org)
Monospace-first, split-pane layout. Signals "creative coding" without corporate polish. Neutral palette, dense information layout, visible tool structure.

2. Playful Pixel Icons

Iconography SVG Craft

What we will do: Design custom pixel art icons for navigation, story metadata, and decorative elements (pilcrows, section markers, category indicators) in the tradition of Susan Kare’s original Macintosh icons, the original NES Legend of Zelda’s item icons, and Undertale’s interface elements.

Already established: The staging site’s navigation buttons are fully custom SVG pixel-art shapes — hand-drawn SVG polygons with a stepped 3D shadow, pixel-perfect chamfered corners, and specific accent colors. This is the craft-object approach in action. The full icon system should extend this vocabulary to all interactive elements. Note: the SVG approach is expressive but fragile — each button is a bespoke file with hard-coded dimensions. The same visual effect (chamfered corners via clip-path: polygon(...), stepped shadow via box-shadow with zero blur or a pseudo-element offset) can be achieved robustly in CSS, making it trivially themeable, responsive, and applicable to any element without per-instance SVG authoring.

Mediating processes

Sense of craft and human care. Pixel art icons communicate that someone invested time in this specific space. They resist the visual language of corporate tech (Material Design, Font Awesome) and signal a community of makers rather than a product. This models a valuing of craft that may carry over into users’ own story-making.

Anti-corporate belonging. The whimsical quality of hand-crafted icons positions the site as a place where idiosyncrasy is valued — where you do not need to be polished or professional to belong. This lowers the stakes for users who might feel intimidated by coding or by “real” writing.

Worldbuilding through iconography. As in Zelda or Undertale, icons carry narrative weight — they are part of the site’s story about itself, contributing to a sense of place and identity across the site.

Susan Kare — Original Macintosh icons (1983–84)
32×32 pixels. Humanistic warmth at tiny scale. The desktop metaphor invented through hand-crafted forms.

Legend of Zelda, NES (1986)
8×8 and 16×16 pixel icons for inventory and UI. Each carries semantic and narrative weight. Players form emotional relationships with these marks.

Undertale (2015)
Retro pixel aesthetics as vehicle for genuine emotion. Interface as narrative. Typography as character.

Staging site: SVG pixel-art button (actual implementation)

The "Start writing" button is a 148×30px SVG polygon with a yellow #ffe564 body, near-black #231F20 shadow polygon offset 6px down-right, and 2px chamfered pixel-art corners. This SVG approach should extend to form inputs, cards, tabs, story player controls — all interactive elements.

3. Immersion / Interactivity Toggle

Interaction Interface Permeability

What we will do: Support oscillation between an immersive story mode (full-focus reading, minimal chrome) and a reflective mode (split-view with source code visible, structural annotations). Implementation approach — explicit toggle, progressive reveal, or story-authored reveal — to be determined through prototyping.

This enacts Ryan’s (2001) immersion/interactivity tension productively: rather than resolving it, the design makes it available as a learning mechanism.

Mediating processes

Oscillating reader/author perspective. When readers can see a story’s source code alongside its rendered form, they simultaneously inhabit reader and author/programmer positions. They discover that the story they are experiencing is also a text they could have written or modified — the “extending into the medium” dimension of permeable media (Proctor et al., 2025).

Critical awareness of constructed interfaces. Making the interface visible at key moments teaches users that all interfaces are designed artifacts with specific affordances and constraints. The habit of asking “how does this work?” is a form of critical computational literacy (Kafai & Proctor, 2021).

Note on Monaspace Neon: Using the same monospace font in both story view and source view means the transition between reading and code feels like zooming out, not switching modes — the story is always already a text that could be read as code.

Three implementation approaches

Option A — Persistent toggle: [◈ source] button always visible; switches between full-width and split view. Simplest to implement.

Option B — Progressive reveal: Story starts immersive; after completion, "how was this made?" prompt offers a replay with source visible.

Option C — Story-authored reveal: Ink authors insert tags that trigger source visibility at dramatic moments. Most sophisticated; requires author tooling.

4. Reader-Writer Relationship UI

Social Community Anti-metrics

What we will do: Design the social architecture to foreground human connections between specific readers and writers rather than aggregated metrics. Author attribution prominent; story pages surface who has read and responded; profiles show reading and writing history together; comments address the author directly; no like counts, follower counts, or trending sections.

Mediating processes

Writing without metrics pressure. When the site does not offer follower counts or like buttons, writers optimize for specific readers rather than for attention-maximizing content. This shifts the social frame from “content creation for an audience” toward “writing for people” — the dialogic, rhetorical view of writing (Bakhtin, 1981; Proctor et al., n.d.).

Authentic community participation. Reader-writer relationships that are personal rather than metric-mediated support an authentic community of practice (Lave & Wenger, 1991). Users are more likely to develop identities as participants in a literary community than as content creators — the situated framing of computational literacies (Kafai & Proctor, 2021).

5. Story Forking / Remix

Community Permeability Participation

What we will do: A “fork this story” affordance creates a copy of any published story in the reader’s workspace, with attribution to the original author. Forked stories display their lineage; a story’s page shows downstream forks, creating a visible family tree of influence. This is a first-class feature, not a footnote.

Forking is the central metaphor of Scratch’s design, where it operationalizes Jenkins et al.’s (2006) concept of participatory culture — a media culture in which participants don’t just consume but actively remix and recirculate. Scratch’s remix culture has been extensively studied (Kafai & Burke 2014; Hill & Monroy-Hernández 2013) as a mechanism by which learners appropriate others’ techniques, transform them, and contribute back — a computational enactment of Bakhtin’s heteroglossia in which every text is composed from and in dialogue with prior texts.

For Unfold Studio, forking directly instantiates the third dimension of permeable media (Proctor et al., 2025): growing beyond the medium. A reader who plays a story and then forks it to change the ending has moved from reader to author using the same text as scaffolding. The fork is the moment of extension.

Mediating processes

Invitation to extend and grow beyond. The visibility of a “fork” button on every story communicates that stories are not finished objects but starting points — that the appropriate response to a story you love is not only to read it again but to take it somewhere. This is a structural affordance for the “growing beyond” dimension of permeable media, making the invitation explicit in the UI rather than leaving it implicit in the site’s philosophy.

Precedent: Scratch (scratch.mit.edu)
Remixing is the central social mechanic of Scratch: every project displays its remix tree, and the platform celebrates derivative works as first-class contributions. @kafai2014a document how remix culture supports both technical learning (appropriating others' code solutions) and identity formation (entering the Scratch figured world as a recognized participant). The remix count is displayed as a community health metric rather than a personal achievement score — a deliberate inversion of social media's individualist metrics.

Precedent: GitHub forks
Fork counts signal community vitality rather than individual status. The fork graph makes the genealogy of code visible — every project exists within a history of derivation. For Unfold Studio, displaying a story's fork lineage creates the same effect: stories as living, evolving objects rather than finished artifacts.

6. Warm, In-Voice Error States

Identity Belonging CS identity

What we will do: When an Ink story fails to compile, the error state is rendered in the site’s voice — a pixel art character, a message that treats the error as a normal moment of craft rather than a failure. The character might be a small creature that appears in the editor when things go wrong, says something specific to the error type, and offers a nudge toward the fix. The tone is warm and matter-of-fact: of course this happens; here’s where to look.

Currently, Ink compilation errors surface as cold technical output — precisely the register that reinforces the stereotype that computing is impersonal, exacting, and not for people who make mistakes. For users who don’t already identify as programmers, an opaque error message at a moment of creative investment is a high-stakes identity challenge: maybe I’m not someone who can do this. The design conjecture is that error states handled in the site’s own voice — humanized, expected, even playful — defuse this and model a different relationship to failure.

Mediating processes

Failure normalized as part of craft. Every writer who codes will encounter compilation errors; every programmer who writes will revise. If the site’s response to errors is consistent with its broader ethos — careful, human, a little whimsical — it communicates that encountering errors is not a sign of being in the wrong place. This is a specific, targeted intervention at one of the highest-anxiety moments in the user journey.

Precedent: Undertale death screen
When the player dies, Undertale does not show a generic "Game Over." It shows a darkened screen, a small flower, and Flowey saying something specific to the context — sometimes mocking, sometimes almost tender. The death state is part of the narrative. The site's error state should be part of the site's narrative.

Precedent: GitHub's 404 / error pages (Octocat)
GitHub's error pages use the Octocat mascot in absurd illustrated scenes. The effect is that encountering an error feels like discovering a hidden part of the site's personality rather than hitting a wall.

Precedent: itch.io error and upload states
itch.io's interface speaks in a warm, slightly informal voice throughout — including error and confirmation states. This consistency creates a sense of a platform with a person behind it, not a service.

7. Seasonal Writing Challenges

Community Identity Not yet implemented

What we will do: Recurring writing events — a monthly prompt, a two-week “story jam,” a back-to-school challenge — each with their own small pixel art visual identity (a badge, an icon, a character). Stories written for a challenge display the challenge’s mark permanently; a “past challenges” archive preserves this history. The site itself visually transforms during a challenge period — a banner, a seasonal palette accent — marking shared time.

This is not yet planned for implementation, but is worth holding as a design horizon. The mechanism it targets — community ritual participation — is not served by any other embodiment in the current map, and it addresses a gap: the figured world of Unfold Studio currently has an ethos (craft, permeability, authentic connection) but not yet a calendar — a shared sense of time that marks participants as members of a community with history and seasons.

Mediating processes

Community ritual participation. Holland et al. (1998) describe figured worlds as constituted partly through artifacts (the pixel art badges, the archive) and partly through activities (writing for a challenge, sharing during a jam period). Recurring events create temporal markers of community membership — you were here for the October jam, you know what the spring prompt was — that are distinct from the spatial markers provided by profile pages and story attribution. Jenkins et al. (2006) document how participatory culture is sustained through these kinds of shared activities rather than through passive consumption.

Precedent: Ludum Dare / itch.io game jams
Game jams create intense short-term community cohesion around a shared constraint. itch.io hosts hundreds of jams; each has its own page, badge, and archive. The jam history is a record of the community's creative seasons.

Precedent: NaNoWriMo
The annual November writing challenge creates community belonging through shared struggle and shared time. The badge system and word-count tracking are ritualized markers of participation, not just metrics. Caution: NaNoWriMo is a useful structural precedent for seasonal challenge design, but has become a contested reference. In 2023 the NaNoWriMo organization published guidance endorsing the use of generative AI for novel-writing, sparking a significant community backlash and public debate about whether AI-assisted writing counts as participation in the challenge. The controversy is directly relevant to Unfold Studio, which also incorporates AI affordances — the site's design should be careful not to replicate NaNoWriMo's framing of AI as an unproblematic writing aid. The seasonal challenges precedent is taken from the structural form of NaNoWriMo (shared time, community, ritualized markers), not from its current stance on AI authorship.

Precedent: Scratch seasonal challenges
Scratch's studio-based seasonal challenges (Halloween animations, holiday cards) drive participation and create a shared archive of community creative history.

8. Visible Story Revision History

CS identity Craft Permeability

What we will do: Every story save is already logged by the platform (the IJCSCL 2026 paper uses this data to classify edit types: text edits, code edits, debugging edits, AI edits). Surface this as a visible timeline on story pages — a compact strip showing the sequence of edit events that produced the story, each clickable to see that version. Authors control visibility; the default for public stories is to show history.

The revision history is a bridge artifact: it is simultaneously a literary object (the manuscript’s evolution, the layers of revision that produced the final text) and a CS object (a commit log, a version history, a diff). Making it visible on story pages normalizes both practices — it says that revision is how writing works, and that tracking changes is how computing works, and that for interactive storytelling these are the same practice.

Mediating processes

Authentic CS patterns at low complexity. A key distinction between permeable media and scaffolding (Pea, 2004) is that scaffolding is a temporary support to be removed, while permeable media uses the same patterns as professional practice, just at reduced complexity. The student who views their story’s edit history is already doing what a programmer does with git log — the pattern is identical, just less powerful. When they later encounter version control in a professional context, they are not learning a new concept; they are seeing a familiar concept with more surface area. The same argument applies to the code editor (already a real code editor), to error messages (already what debugging looks like), and to the split source/preview view (already what an IDE feels like). This is what makes the medium permeable: the student does not have to leave their Unfold Studio practices behind when they grow through it. Surfacing story revision history as a natural feature of the writing interface makes this practice legible in a literary frame — not “this is a CS tool” but “this is how you can see how the story was made.” Writers who encounter this for their own stories may develop an understanding of version control as a writing practice before encountering it as a CS concept.

Precedent: Wikipedia revision history
Every article's history is publicly visible and linked from the article itself. The revision history is treated as part of the article — a record of the community's collective authorship. "View history" is a first-class tab, not a buried admin feature.

Precedent: Google Docs version history
"See version history" is surfaced as a named feature. Versions can be named ("after first draft"), creating a narrative of the document's development. Writers who use Google Docs regularly develop an intuitive understanding of version control without calling it that.

Precedent: git log / GitHub commit history
The commit graph is how programmers read the history of a project. Unfold Studio's edit timeline is a story's commit log — making this parallel explicit bridges the two practices.

9. Embedded Media Genre UI

Critical literacy Dialogism Partially implemented

What we will do: Provide UI components that allow story authors to embed recognizable digital media genres within their narratives — text message conversations, social media post frames, chat interfaces, news article excerpts. SMS/text message speech bubbles are already supported; future extensions will include Instagram-style post frames, tweet-format components, and other visual artifacts of social media genres.

The mechanism is Bakhtinian: the novel has always had the capacity to incorporate, stylize, and put into dialogue other genres — speech genres, social registers, discourse types. Interactive storytelling inherits and extends this capacity to digital media. When a story embeds an SMS conversation, it does what Bakhtin described as “novelizing” a genre: fixing and stylizing it, making it “permeated with laughter, irony, humor, elements of self-parody” (Bakhtin, 1981). The reader simultaneously recognizes the genre and sees it held at critical distance — which is exactly the condition needed for analysis.

This connects directly to the site’s critical possibilities. The dissertation (Proctor, 2020) documents how participants in early Unfold Studio workshops were drawn to embedding text messaging and social media speech genres in their stories — modeling bilingual conversations, microaggressions, social positioning. These stories worked as critical discourse models: simulations of social realities that make otherwise-invisible discourse dynamics visible and available for analysis. The visual genre component (the speech bubble that looks like iMessage) is not incidental to this effect; it is the trigger for recognition and the mechanism for critical distance.

Mediating processes

Ironic framing of embedded genres. Seeing a familiar genre inside an interactive story creates a productive double-awareness: the reader recognizes the genre and its conventions while also knowing it is authored and subject to the story’s intent. This double-awareness is the condition for critical analysis — it transforms a form of media one passively inhabits into an object one can examine. The key design principle is that embedded genre components should be recognizable but clearly constructed — close enough to trigger recognition, not so accurate that they are mistaken for the real thing. The slight parody is not a failure of fidelity; it is the critical mechanism.

Precedent: Coming Out Simulator — Nicky Case (2014)
Uses a text message interface that mimics a mobile phone UI. The recognition mechanism is central to the game's effect: you understand immediately what kind of conversation this is, what the stakes are, and what the genre normally permits. The game then uses those conventions to create dramatic constraint and to model how identity is negotiated within familiar discourse genres. The interface IS the argument.

Precedent: Early Unfold Studio workshops [@proctor2019a]
Workshop participants spontaneously requested the ability to embed text messaging speech bubbles and emoji. The paper describes this as appropriating "speech genres into their own storytelling," evoking Bakhtin's analysis of how the novel fixes and stylizes other genres while making them more flexible and ironic. Several participants wrote stories that modeled bilingual code-switching, using the SMS genre to simulate how register and identity interact in real conversations.
